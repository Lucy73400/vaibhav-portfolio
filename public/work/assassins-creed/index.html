02

The
Idea

What if the opening title of a game — the moment before the world lets you in — could be felt as deeply as a film?

Assassin's Creed was never just a video game. To me, it was the first piece of visual storytelling that made me understand that atmosphere is a language. The hidden blade reveal. The eagle cry. The sense that you are stepping into history with a purpose.

This project began as a personal challenge: to recreate that feeling from scratch, in my own visual language. No game assets. No shortcuts. The goal was to animate an opening sequence that captures the emotional weight of the Creed — the secrecy, the blade, the leap of faith.

I wanted to explore how far I could push illustration and motion design together — turning still drawings into a scene that breathes, moves, and carries a story without a single line of dialogue.

05 — The Making Of

The animation did not
appear instantly.

Seven stages. Each one building on the last. The final film is the sum of every decision made in each stage below.

i
Concept
Thumbnails, story beats, emotional arc. What must the viewer feel at the end?
ii
Illustration
Drawing all assets in Procreate — character, environment, the blade, the eagle.
iii
Asset Prep
Separating illustration layers for animation. Each part of the frame must move independently.
iv
Animation
Keyframing in After Effects. Timing, easing, the weight of the blade reveal.
v
Compositing
Layering — particle effects, light leaks, depth of field, atmospheric haze.
vi
Editing
Cutting the sequence together. Pacing, scene transitions, the final rhythm.
vii
Final Polish
Colour grade, grain, title treatment. The difference between finished and cinematic.
Stage ii — Illustration

Every layer was drawn to move

Before a single keyframe was set, each illustration element was drawn on a separate Procreate layer — knowing that arm, blade, cape, and city would all need to move independently in After Effects.

Stage iv — Animation

Keyframe by keyframe

In After Effects, each element was keyframed separately. The hidden blade reveal was the most important timing decision — too fast and the moment is lost, too slow and the tension deflates.

Stage v — Compositing

Atmosphere is not a filter.
It is a decision.

The final atmosphere — grain, warm particle dust, colour grade — was applied last. These elements push the animation from "illustrated" into "cinematic." The grain especially closes the gap between drawn and filmed.

Colour grade — shadows shifted warm, highlights kept clean
Grain overlay — 35mm film grain at 8–12% opacity
Particle dust — floating motes of light, ancient atmosphere
Vignette — draws the eye to the blade, every time

08

Looking
back

A personal reflection

This project taught me that the gap between what you imagine and what you can execute is exactly where growth lives. My mental version of this animation was perfect. The real version forced me to make hundreds of small decisions I hadn't anticipated.

The most challenging part wasn't the animation itself — it was the timing of the blade reveal. I must have keyframed it fifteen different ways. Too fast, and the magic evaporates. Too slow, and the tension dies. The right moment is somewhere between a breath and a heartbeat.

What I would do differently: plan the sound design from the beginning, not as an afterthought. The audio shapes the timing of every visual choice, and I realised this too late in the process.

i
Atmosphere is built in post, not planned in post. The grain and colour grade need to be considered from the very first illustration.
ii
Fan projects are the best training. When there's no client, the only critic is your own taste — which is the hardest critic to satisfy.
iii
This project confirmed something: I want to direct animation that creates this feeling — not for a game, not for a brand, but for a story of my own.
iv
The journey doesn't begin with the project. It begins the moment you decide to make something difficult, alone, with no guarantee it will work.
